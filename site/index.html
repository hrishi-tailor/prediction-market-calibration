Are prediction markets calibrated?

When a market prices an outcome at 70¢, does it happen 70% of the time? I tested 46,168 resolved markets from Kalshi and Polymarket, pricing each one 30 days, 7 days, 1 day and 1 hour before it closed.

Mostly, yes: realized frequencies sit within 1–2 points of prices on average. But Kalshi has a favourite–longshot bias: cheap contracts are overpriced, the bias grows as close approaches, and it lives in thinly traded markets. Polymarket shows no such bias. Neither mispricing survives trading costs.

Prices against reality

Each point groups markets by price and shows how often they actually resolved Yes. On a perfectly calibrated market every point sits on the dashed diagonal. Bars are 95% confidence intervals, clustered by event, because markets in the same event resolve together.

The favourite–longshot bias

One number summarises calibration: the slope b in a logistic regression of outcomes on prices, P(Yes) = sigmoid(a + b·logit(price)). A slope of 1 is perfect. Above 1, longshots are overpriced and favourites underpriced; below 1, prices are overconfident. The slope doesn't depend on which side of a market is labelled Yes.

Where the bias lives

Measured one day before close. On Kalshi, the least-traded third of markets carries the bias and the most-traded third is calibrated. On Polymarket the busiest markets lean the other way, toward overconfidence.

Can you make money from it?

No. Trading at the quoted price on Kalshi a day before close, buying Yes at the ask or No at one minus the bid, and paying the taker fee: cheap contracts lose heavily on either side, and favourites lose only the spread and fee. No price bucket earns a significantly positive return.

How I know it's real

Three things can fake a favourite–longshot bias or a calibration error. Each was checked explicitly.

Tick size

Whole-cent quotes compress extreme prices toward 50¢. Refitting on 5–95¢ prices only, Kalshi's one-day slope is still 1.08 (95% CI 1.02–1.14).

Stopping time

"Will X happen by D?" markets close when X happens, so their close time depends on the outcome. 10% of Polymarket markets closed early; without them its one-day slope is 0.93, so the conclusion holds.

Cost of capital

Money locked in a contract also pulls prices toward 50¢, but at 5% a year by at most 0.21¢ over 30 days. That's far too small to explain the bias.

Method

  • Prices before close, not final prices. By close the outcome is usually known, so final prices are trivially "calibrated". Only data from before each horizon is used.
  • Kalshi: bid/ask midpoints from hourly candles, with spreads over 10¢ dropped. Polymarket: hourly prices for the two weeks before close, coarser before that.
  • Sampling caps: at most 10 markets per series or event, so a few hourly weather and index series can't dominate. All sampling is seeded.
  • Uncertainty clustered by event: a cluster bootstrap for the reliability tables and cluster-robust standard errors for the regressions.

Limitations

  • Lifetime volume is measured after the fact, so "high volume" means markets that ended up liquid, not liquidity at the moment of the price.
  • Kalshi doesn't expose a scheduled close separate from the actual one, so the stopping-time check covers Polymarket only.
  • The two platforms list very different markets, so their Brier scores aren't directly comparable.
